# 00 Principles & Governance

This section defines the constitution of AI-SDLC: the principles that govern Human/AI ownership, decision rights, accountability, quality responsibility and acceptable levels of AI autonomy.

## Preamble

**We, IT engineers**, aware of the opportunities that Generative AI brings to software engineering, and at the same time responsible for the quality, security, maintainability and long-term value of the systems we create, establish the principles for the conscious and responsible use of GenAI throughout the software development lifecycle.

We recognize GenAI as a tool for **augmenting human capability rather than transferring engineering responsibility to a machine**. Automation may take over mechanical, repetitive and time-consuming work; however, understanding the problem, making material engineering decisions, assessing risk and taking responsibility for the outcome remain human responsibilities.

We strive for a model of Human–AI collaboration that increases the speed and quality of software delivery without causing the team to lose knowledge, engineering capability or **cognitive ownership** of the solution.

Therefore, we use AI where it can reduce engineering toil, improve the quality of analysis, automate verification and accelerate the execution of clearly defined tasks. We preserve active human involvement wherever business context, engineering judgement, design, decision-making or risk acceptance are required.

We accept that **responsibility for software cannot be delegated to an AI model**. Every artifact generated or modified with the assistance of AI must have a human or team that understands its meaning, can justify the decisions behind it and accepts responsibility for its use.

We establish this AI-Augmented Software Development Lifecycle as a **living operating model**, subject to measurement, evaluation and continuous evolution as technologies, tools and team experience with GenAI change.

The principles emerging from this preamble are: **augmentation > replacement**, **human accountability**, **cognitive ownership**, **risk-based AI autonomy**, and **continuous evaluation**.

## Article 1 — Human Ownership & Accountability

### 1.1 Every material change must have a human owner

Every material change introduced through AI-SDLC must have an identifiable human owner or accountable team. This applies to requirements, designs, source code, tests, configuration, infrastructure changes, architectural decisions, release decisions and other artifacts that may affect the behavior or operation of the system.

**No artifact becomes ownerless because AI participated in its creation.**

### 1.2 AI assistance does not transfer accountability

Delegating analysis, documentation, implementation, testing, review or other work to GenAI does not delegate responsibility for the outcome. Accountability remains with the human role, team or decision owner responsible for accepting and using the result.

The statement “AI generated it” is never a valid substitute for engineering accountability.

### 1.3 Ownership concerns the outcome, not authorship volume

Human ownership does not require a person to manually create every line of code, test, document or configuration. AI may perform a substantial part of the execution.

What remains non-transferable is responsibility for the resulting engineering outcome: whether the artifact is accepted, whether it is fit for purpose and whether it may safely proceed to the next lifecycle stage.

### 1.4 Human acceptance is required at accountable gates

At every lifecycle gate where acceptance, approval or risk commitment is required, the responsible human role must remain identifiable.

Depending on the lifecycle stage and risk class, such gates may include:

* analysis approval,
* architecture approval,
* Ready for Development,
* pull request approval,
* functional or business acceptance,
* Ready for Production,
* production GO / NO-GO,
* rollback or roll-forward decisions,
* release acceptance.

The accountable owner may change between lifecycle stages. AI-SDLC does not require one person to own the entire lifecycle, but it does require **clear human accountability at every material decision point**.

### 1.5 AI may recommend; humans accept material decisions

GenAI may analyze options, identify risks, propose alternatives and recommend actions. Where a decision materially affects architecture, domain behavior, security, data, public contracts, operational risk, business risk or production behavior, acceptance of that decision remains a human responsibility.

AI may support the decision process. **It may not become the accountable decision-maker.**

### 1.6 AI output is not trusted by origin alone

An artifact does not become trusted merely because it was generated by an advanced model, passed an AI review or was produced by a successful autonomous agent run.

AI-generated and AI-modified artifacts must pass the validation and approval controls required by their nature and assigned risk class before they are treated as trusted engineering outputs.

### 1.7 Production accountability remains human

For material production changes, decisions concerning risk acceptance, deployment continuation, rollout interruption, rollback, roll-forward and final release acceptance remain human-owned.

Automation may execute these actions where explicitly authorized by policy, but **accountability for the policy, thresholds and decision authority remains human**.

### 1.8 Accountability must be traceable

For material lifecycle artifacts and gates, it must be possible to determine who or which accountable role accepted the outcome and allowed it to proceed.

This traceability exists to preserve engineering responsibility, enable learning from failures and prevent responsibility from becoming obscured behind automation or AI-generated work.

> **Constitutional principle:** AI may perform the work. A human must own the outcome.

## Article 2 — Cognitive Ownership & Human Understanding

### 2.1 Human ownership requires sufficient understanding

Formal accountability alone is not sufficient.

A person responsible for an engineering outcome must possess a sufficient mental model of the solution to understand what it does, why it was designed in that way, how it interacts with the surrounding system and what may happen when it fails.

**Ownership without understanding is not considered effective engineering ownership.**

### 2.2 AI must not reduce the engineer to a passive reviewer

AI-SDLC must not create a development model in which engineers primarily receive large volumes of AI-generated work and limit their role to superficial inspection and approval.

The purpose of GenAI is to augment engineering capability, not to replace active participation in problem solving, design and technical reasoning.

A developer responsible for a change should remain an active participant in its creation, even when substantial parts of the implementation are delegated to AI.

### 2.3 The required level of understanding depends on impact and risk

The depth of cognitive ownership required must be proportional to the importance and risk of the change.

For low-risk or mechanical work, understanding of the local behavior and contracts may be sufficient.

For changes affecting domain behavior, architecture, security, data consistency, concurrency, critical integrations or production reliability, the responsible engineer must understand the wider consequences and relevant trade-offs.

The R0–R4 risk model defines how much human understanding and validation is required.

### 2.4 The engineer must understand the intent before reviewing the implementation

Whenever practical, the responsible engineer should know the intended behavior, constraints and validation criteria **before** AI-generated implementation is reviewed.

Reviewing code without prior knowledge of the intended design increases the risk of accepting a locally plausible implementation without understanding whether it is the correct solution.

Therefore, AI-SDLC prefers the sequence:

**understand → design → define constraints → delegate → review**

over:

**delegate → inspect generated result → accept**

### 2.5 The engineer must be able to explain the solution

A responsible engineer must be able to explain, at a level appropriate to the risk class:

* what problem the change solves,
* how the solution works,
* which components and contracts are involved,
* which important assumptions were made,
* which engineering decisions were taken,
* which alternatives or trade-offs were considered,
* what the main failure modes are,
* how correctness was verified,
* what operational or maintenance consequences may follow.

This does not require memorizing implementation detail. It requires understanding the engineering meaning of the change.

### 2.6 Significant AI-made decisions must be visible

If an AI agent makes implementation decisions that were not explicitly defined by a human, those decisions must be identifiable during review.

The agent should surface assumptions, deviations, additional decisions and unresolved risks rather than hiding them inside the resulting code.

The more significant the decision, the stronger the requirement for explicit human review and acceptance.

### 2.7 Inability to explain a material change is a quality signal

If the responsible engineer cannot adequately explain or defend a material implementation, this must be treated as evidence that the change has not yet reached the required level of engineering ownership.

Passing tests, static analysis, AI review or CI/CD does not compensate for the absence of sufficient human understanding.

For higher-risk changes, insufficient understanding is itself a reason to block approval.

### 2.8 Human review must preserve independent reasoning

AI-generated explanations, summaries and review comments may assist understanding, but they must not replace independent human reasoning.

Where appropriate, reviewers should first inspect the change and form their own view before being exposed to detailed explanations from the author or AI. This reduces anchoring and preserves independent engineering judgement.

### 2.9 AI-SDLC must protect long-term engineering capability

Efficiency gains from GenAI must not systematically weaken the team's ability to design, debug, maintain and evolve software without relying on opaque generated output.

The organization should therefore monitor whether AI usage improves productivity while preserving:

* system knowledge,
* debugging ability,
* architectural understanding,
* domain knowledge,
* decision-making capability,
* ability to explain and maintain existing code.

A process that delivers software faster while progressively removing understanding from the engineering team is not considered successful AI augmentation.

> **Constitutional principle:** AI may accelerate implementation. Humans must retain the mental model of the system.

## Article 3 — Human Decision Authority

### 3.1 Material engineering decisions remain human decisions

GenAI may analyze problems, present alternatives, identify risks, simulate consequences and recommend solutions.

However, where a decision may materially affect system behavior, architecture, security, data, business risk or operational capability, **decision authority remains with a human**.

AI may support the decision-making process. It may not replace the accountable decision owner.

### 3.2 An AI recommendation is not a decision

The result of a model analysis, an agent recommendation or an automatically generated plan does not acquire the status of an engineering decision merely because it is presented as the most likely or optimal solution.

A material decision requires conscious acceptance by a person or role with the appropriate competence and mandate.

**Recommendation is not authorization.**

### 3.3 A Material Decision requires Human Decision Authority

A **Material Decision** includes, in particular, a decision affecting one or more of the following areas:

* business behavior of the system,
* domain model or its invariants,
* boundaries of components, services or bounded contexts,
* solution architecture,
* public APIs, events or other contracts,
* data model and significant data migrations,
* transaction and data-consistency model,
* concurrency and synchronization,
* security, authorization or permissions,
* privacy and data-processing rules,
* critical external dependencies,
* significant non-functional requirements,
* deployment strategy,
* production risk,
* rollback, roll-forward or rollout interruption,
* acceptance of known risk or deviation from an established standard.

This list is not exhaustive. The materiality of a decision is determined primarily by its **potential impact and reversibility**, not by the number of changed lines of code.

### 3.4 Decision Authority must be assigned to the appropriate role

Not every decision requires approval by the same person.

AI-SDLC assumes distributed decision authority aligned with competence and lifecycle stage.

Depending on the nature of the decision, authority may belong, for example, to:

* a Product Owner or business representative,
* an analyst,
* a developer,
* a Tech Lead,
* an architect,
* a Security Engineer,
* a QA/Test Lead,
* an Engineering Manager,
* a Release Owner,
* an Incident Commander.

The objective is not to centralize all decisions, but to ensure that **for every Material Decision it is clear who has the authority to make it**.

### 3.5 Decisions should precede delegated execution

Where a material decision can be identified before work is delegated to AI, it should be made by a human before execution begins.

The preferred model is:

**context → human decision → constraints → AI execution**

rather than:

**AI execution → implicit decision hidden in implementation → human discovery during review**

The purpose is to keep AI in the role of executor or advisor where decision authority is reserved for humans.

### 3.6 AI must not independently redefine business intent

AI must not decide that a requirement, acceptance criterion, Definition of Done or previously accepted contract “should mean something else” merely because another interpretation is technically easier or better fits the generated implementation.

Where a conflict exists between:

* a requirement and the implementation,
* a specification and the existing system,
* a test and application behavior,
* architecture and a locally preferable solution,

AI should surface the conflict and refer the decision to the appropriate Human Decision Owner.

### 3.7 Uncertainty about decision authority requires escalation

If an agent cannot clearly determine whether a decision falls within its delegated execution scope or constitutes a Material Decision, it should not make that decision implicitly.

Where material uncertainty exists, the preferred behavior is:

**STOP → DESCRIBE → PROPOSE → ESCALATE**

The agent should present:

* the problem encountered,
* the decision required,
* available options,
* expected consequences,
* its own recommendation where useful.

The final decision remains with the authorized human.

### 3.8 Local implementation decisions may be delegated

Human Decision Authority does not require manual approval of every local programming decision.

AI may independently make reversible, local and low-risk implementation decisions when they:

* remain within the accepted scope of the task,
* do not change defined contracts,
* do not violate established architectural rules,
* do not alter functional intent,
* do not materially increase risk,
* remain consistent with applicable project standards.

The scope of this autonomy is defined in more detail by **Article 4 — AI Autonomy & Delegation Boundaries** and the R0–R4 risk model.

### 3.9 The harder a decision is to reverse, the stronger the human control required

The required level of Human Decision Authority should increase with:

* the impact of the decision,
* the cost of failure,
* the difficulty of detecting failure,
* the difficulty of reversing its effects,
* the blast radius,
* the significance of the affected data or business process.

In particular, decisions that may produce irreversible or costly consequences should not result solely from autonomous AI action.

### 3.10 AI may challenge a human decision

Preserving Human Decision Authority does not mean AI should blindly execute every instruction.

If AI identifies:

* a material risk,
* a contradiction,
* a potential security problem,
* a violation of previously accepted principles,
* a possibility of data loss,
* a high probability of failure,

it should explicitly raise the concern and provide its reasoning.

After the risk has been surfaced, **decision authority remains with the appropriate human**, unless another security or compliance policy requires the process to stop unconditionally.

> **Constitutional principle:** AI may analyze, recommend and challenge. Material decisions remain human.

## Article 4 — AI Autonomy & Delegation Boundaries

### 4.1 AI autonomy is delegated, not assumed

An AI agent does not possess autonomy by default.

Its authority to act derives from an explicit delegation defined by a human, an approved process or an established policy.

**Absence of explicit authorization does not imply permission.**

The default operating model is therefore not “do whatever is necessary”, but rather **“act independently within clearly defined boundaries.”**

### 4.2 Every delegation should have a defined scope

For material work, an agent should receive an execution contract that defines, as appropriate:

* the objective,
* input context,
* allowed scope,
* constraints,
* artifacts or areas it may modify,
* artifacts or areas that are excluded from modification,
* established contracts,
* requirements and invariants,
* completion criteria,
* required validations,
* stop and escalation conditions.

Low-risk R0 work does not necessarily require a formal document, but **delegation boundaries must be understandable to both the human and the AI**.

### 4.3 AI may act independently within approved boundaries

Within the delegated scope, AI should be allowed to perform real work autonomously.

This may include, where permitted:

* creating and modifying implementation code,
* performing local refactoring,
* generating boilerplate,
* implementing accepted tests,
* running development tools,
* analyzing failures,
* iteratively correcting implementation,
* making local implementation decisions permitted under Article 3.

The objective is not to require human approval for every agent action.

**Autonomy should be real within the boundary and restrictive at the boundary.**

### 4.4 AI must not silently expand scope

If completing a task requires a change outside the delegated scope, the agent must not perform that change automatically merely because it would simplify completion.

This applies in particular to changes affecting:

* public contracts,
* functional requirements,
* architecture,
* domain boundaries,
* data schema or semantics,
* security,
* material configuration,
* new dependencies,
* infrastructure,
* accepted project standards.

Such a discovery is an input back to the human decision process, not an automatic extension of the agent’s mandate.

### 4.5 Crossing a delegation boundary requires STOP → DESCRIBE → PROPOSE → ESCALATE

When an agent encounters work requiring authority beyond the delegated scope, it must:

**STOP** — pause the affected part of the work,

**DESCRIBE** — explain the issue and why the current mandate is insufficient,

**PROPOSE** — present reasonable options and, where useful, a recommendation,

**ESCALATE** — request a decision from the appropriate human owner.

After human authorization, the delegation may be expanded and execution may continue.

### 4.6 AI must not redefine success criteria in order to achieve success

If an agent cannot satisfy the defined completion criteria, it must not resolve the problem by independently changing:

* requirements,
* expected outcomes,
* acceptance criteria,
* Definition of Done,
* accepted test intent,
* material contracts.

**The agent may change the solution. It may not independently move the goalposts by which the solution is judged.**

### 4.7 Technical permissions should match delegated authority

Logical autonomy and technical access should remain aligned.

An agent should receive only the capabilities and permissions required to perform its delegated work.

This includes control over, where relevant:

* repository write access,
* command execution,
* network access,
* secrets,
* environments,
* production data,
* deployment capabilities,
* infrastructure modification.

The principle of least privilege applies to AI agents as it does to human and service identities.

Detailed security requirements are defined separately in the AI-SDLC security and compliance rules.

### 4.8 An agent may not delegate more authority than it has received

If an agent uses a sub-agent, skill, external tool or another model, the delegated authority of that downstream component must not exceed the authority originally granted to the parent agent.

Human-granted scope may be narrowed through further delegation, but it must not be expanded by it.

**Delegated authority is transitive only within the original boundary.**

### 4.9 Autonomy must be proportional to risk

The permitted level of AI autonomy should decrease as the risk and materiality of the work increase.

As a general direction:

* **R0** may allow broad execution autonomy for trivial or mechanical work,
* **R1–R2** require increasingly explicit scope and human decision gates,
* **R3–R4** require strongly constrained autonomy and greater human control over critical decisions and execution stages.

The detailed mapping between risk class and permitted autonomy is defined in the R0–R4 Risk Model.

### 4.10 Autonomy ends when the delegated objective or stop condition is reached

An agent should not continue improving or restructuring the system after the delegated objective has been completed.

Additional refactoring, dependency updates or adjacent improvements may be proposed, but they should be treated as new scope rather than performed opportunistically.

This prevents agentic scope creep and keeps execution aligned with the intended change.

### 4.11 Agent output should disclose actions, decisions and deviations

After completing material delegated work, the agent should provide an execution summary that includes at least:

* what was changed,
* which validations were executed,
* whether all completion criteria were satisfied,
* which local implementation decisions were made autonomously,
* any deviations, unresolved issues or risks,
* relevant work that was deliberately not performed.

This forms the basis of the **Agent Completion Report** and supports later human review and traceability.

### 4.12 Human authority to constrain or revoke autonomy is continuous

Delegated AI autonomy is always revocable.

A Human Owner or applicable policy may at any time:

* stop execution,
* narrow the scope,
* reduce permissions,
* require additional validation,
* take over the work manually.

This is especially appropriate when unexpected behavior, increasing risk, repeated failure, non-deterministic behavior or loss of confidence in the current execution is observed.

> **Constitutional principle:** AI autonomy is bounded authority. Within approved boundaries AI may act independently; beyond them it must stop and escalate.

## Article 5 — Human-Defined Correctness & Validation

### 5.1 Correctness is defined by humans

Humans, or the accountable human team, define what correct system behavior means.

Sources of this definition may include:

* business requirements,
* acceptance criteria,
* domain rules and invariants,
* public and internal contracts,
* Definition of Done,
* relevant non-functional requirements.

AI may assist in refining these definitions, detecting contradictions and proposing missing scenarios, but it must not independently establish the success criteria for a solution it is also expected to implement.

**AI may help define correctness. Humans authorize what correctness means.**

### 5.2 Correctness should be defined before implementation where reasonably possible

Before implementation begins, the team should know enough about expected behavior to explain how the change will be recognized as correct.

This normally includes the critical scenarios, principal happy paths, important negative paths, key business rules, known failure modes and relevant non-functional expectations.

Detailed manual scripts, final test data, every technical edge case and complete regression coverage do not need to exist before implementation.

**Implementation should not begin before the team can explain how the change will be recognized as correct.**

### 5.3 Test intent belongs to humans

Humans remain responsible for defining the intent of validation, including:

* what is being verified,
* why it is important,
* which behavior is expected,
* which scenarios are critical.

AI may perform substantial test mechanics, including implementing unit, integration, API or E2E tests, generating fixtures and mocks, preparing test data, executing tests and analyzing failures.

The preferred division is:

**Human → test intent**  
**AI → test mechanics**  
**Human → acceptance**

### 5.4 AI must not change success criteria to make its implementation pass

If an implementation does not satisfy an accepted requirement, expected result, acceptance criterion, Definition of Done, test intent or material contract, AI must not resolve the mismatch by unilaterally changing the expected outcome.

Such a situation is a **specification / implementation mismatch** and must be surfaced to a human decision owner.

The appropriate response is:

**STOP → REPORT MISMATCH → PROPOSE OPTIONS → HUMAN DECISION**

A human may then decide whether the implementation, test, requirement or contract must change.

### 5.5 Tests are evidence of correctness, not the definition of truth

Passing tests provide evidence that the system behaves according to the scenarios and assertions that have been defined.

They do not prove that the specification itself is complete or correct.

A green pipeline may still coexist with:

* missing scenarios,
* incorrect assumptions,
* incomplete acceptance criteria,
* incorrect test oracles,
* emergent system behavior,
* unanticipated failure modes.

**Passing tests are necessary evidence where required, but they are not proof that the specification itself is correct or complete.**

### 5.6 Validation should use independent layers

Correctness should not rely on a single validation mechanism.

Depending on the nature and risk of the change, evidence may include:

* unit tests,
* component tests,
* integration tests,
* contract tests,
* API tests,
* E2E tests,
* manual acceptance,
* exploratory testing,
* static analysis,
* security testing,
* performance testing,
* resilience testing,
* production observability and telemetry.

AI-SDLC therefore favors **defence in depth** rather than maximizing any single test layer. In particular, E2E automation should be selective and should not replace the broader test pyramid.

### 5.7 Validation must not rely solely on the same AI that produced the artifact

AI may review and validate work it helped generate, but for material changes the producer must not be the only source of confidence in its own output.

Independent validation mechanisms should be introduced as appropriate, such as deterministic tests, static tooling, separate review context, another reviewer and human validation.

The objective is not necessarily to use a different model, but to ensure that **the generator is not the sole judge of its own result**.

### 5.8 New knowledge may legitimately change the correctness contract

Accepted tests, requirements and validation criteria are not immutable when new information is discovered.

Implementation, integration or exploratory work may expose:

* missing requirements,
* incorrect assumptions,
* previously unknown edge cases,
* domain contradictions,
* legacy-system behavior,
* integration limitations.

In such cases the correctness contract may be changed through an explicit human decision:

**new information → human review → change accepted → correctness contract updated → implementation continues**

The rule is therefore not that tests may never change, but that **AI may not unilaterally change an accepted correctness contract**.

### 5.9 Manual and exploratory validation remain valid engineering tools

Automation is not an objective in itself.

Human validation remains appropriate for areas such as usability, business sense, unexpected combinations of behavior, emergent failures and exploratory investigation.

AI may assist by generating test charters, scenarios or checklists, but manual and exploratory testing remain legitimate forms of engineering evidence rather than failures of automation.

### 5.10 Non-functional correctness is part of correctness

A system may satisfy its functional requirements and still be unacceptable if it fails important non-functional expectations.

Where relevant, correctness therefore also includes properties such as:

* security,
* performance,
* resilience,
* scalability,
* concurrency behavior,
* observability,
* recoverability,
* compatibility.

The required depth of non-functional validation depends on the nature and risk of the change.

### 5.11 Validation depth is proportional to risk

The amount, independence and depth of validation must increase with the materiality and risk of the change.

Low-risk work may be sufficiently validated through lightweight automated controls, while higher-risk work may require multiple independent test layers, non-functional validation, explicit human acceptance and stronger production safeguards.

The detailed mapping is defined by the R0–R4 Risk Model.

### 5.12 Correctness continues into production

Validation does not end when CI is green or when pre-production acceptance is complete.

After deployment, production smoke tests, telemetry, error rates, operational indicators, business KPIs and unexpected system behavior provide the final validation layer.

Production observation therefore forms part of the evidence that a release is truly operating correctly.

> **Constitutional principle:** Humans define what correct means. AI may implement and automate validation, but it may not redefine correctness to validate its own result.

## Article 6 — Risk-Based AI Autonomy

### 6.1 AI autonomy must be proportional to risk

There is no single appropriate level of AI autonomy for all software development work.

The scope of delegation, required human involvement and strength of validation controls must be proportional to the risk of the specific change.

**The higher the risk, the narrower the AI autonomy and the stronger the independent controls.**

### 6.2 Risk is determined by impact, not implementation size

Risk classification must focus on the potential consequences of a change rather than superficial implementation metrics such as lines of code.

A small change to authorization logic may carry critical risk, while thousands of lines of generated test code may remain low risk.

Risk assessment should consider, as applicable:

* potential business impact,
* blast radius,
* significance and sensitivity of affected data,
* security and privacy implications,
* cost of failure,
* detectability of failure,
* reversibility of the change,
* impact on dependent systems,
* criticality of the business process,
* regulatory or contractual obligations,
* impact on availability, integrity and recoverability.

**Lines of code are not a risk metric.**

### 6.3 AI-SDLC uses a common R0–R4 risk model

AI-SDLC uses a common cross-cutting classification:

* **R0 — trivial / mechanical**
* **R1 — standard**
* **R2 — significant**
* **R3 — high risk**
* **R4 — critical**

The detailed criteria, examples and mandatory controls for each class are defined in the R0–R4 Risk Model.

### 6.4 Risk classification affects the entire lifecycle

Risk class does not determine only how much code AI may generate.

It must influence, where applicable:

* depth of analysis,
* architecture rigor,
* level of human design,
* permitted AI autonomy,
* formality of the execution contract,
* test intent and test depth,
* non-functional validation,
* review requirements,
* pair review or specialist review,
* number and strength of human gates,
* deployment strategy,
* release approval,
* observability requirements,
* recovery and rollback strategy.

Risk therefore acts as a cross-cutting control dimension for the complete AI-SDLC.

### 6.5 Risk classification is human-owned

AI may analyze risk factors, identify hazards and recommend a risk class.

For material changes, final classification remains a human-owned decision performed by the appropriate accountable role.

AI may recommend and explain. Humans classify and accept.

### 6.6 Uncertainty must not silently lower the risk class

Uncertainty about whether a change belongs to a higher or lower risk class must not be used to justify weaker controls by default.

Where uncertainty is material, the issue should be escalated or temporarily handled using the more restrictive applicable controls until a human decision is made.

**Uncertainty is not permission to weaken safeguards.**

### 6.7 Risk classification may change during the lifecycle

Risk classification is not a permanent label assigned once at the beginning of work.

New information discovered during analysis, implementation, integration, testing or release preparation may materially change the nature of the change.

When this occurs, the risk class must be reassessed and the required controls updated accordingly.

### 6.8 Increasing risk may invalidate previous delegation

If work initially delegated under a lower risk class reveals changes or dependencies that materially increase risk, the original AI mandate does not automatically expand to cover the new risk.

Risk escalation may require:

* stopping the affected work,
* reassessing the change,
* narrowing or redefining the AI scope,
* adding human decision gates,
* introducing additional validation,
* explicit re-authorization before execution continues.

**Risk escalation may require re-authorization of AI autonomy.**

### 6.9 Multiple individually small changes may create material combined risk

Risk must be evaluated not only at the level of individual tasks or commits, but also at the level of the combined change and release.

Several individually low-risk modifications may create significant compound risk when deployed together, for example through the interaction of dependency updates, configuration changes, migrations, feature flags and infrastructure changes.

AI-SDLC must therefore account for **compound release risk**.

### 6.10 Higher risk requires greater independence of validation

As risk increases, confidence should come from increasingly independent validation mechanisms rather than simply from a larger number of checks performed by the same actor or agent.

Higher-risk work may therefore require combinations of:

* deterministic automated testing,
* static and security tooling,
* independent AI review context,
* human review,
* specialist review,
* explicit acceptance,
* controlled rollout,
* production observation.

The objective is to increase the number of **independent sources of confidence**.

### 6.11 Risk controls must reduce real risk, not become ceremonial

Risk controls exist to reduce specific failure modes and consequences.

A required review, test or approval must represent a meaningful control appropriate to the risk rather than a checkbox performed only to satisfy process formality.

AI-SDLC should actively avoid **compliance theatre**.

Controls that repeatedly create cost without providing meaningful risk reduction should be evaluated, redesigned or removed.

### 6.12 Delivery pressure does not automatically reduce required controls

Deadlines, incidents, customer pressure or delivery urgency do not by themselves reduce the risk classification or silently waive required safeguards.

Urgency may justify an explicit exception through the approved governance process, but it must remain visible, owned and consciously accepted.

**Urgency may justify an explicit exception. It does not silently redefine risk.**

### 6.13 Risk controls must evolve based on evidence

The R0–R4 model and the controls associated with it must be treated as an evolving system.

Evidence from delivery metrics, escaped defects, incidents, review outcomes, agent failures, rework and operational experience should be used to determine whether existing controls are appropriately calibrated.

Risk-based governance must not become bureaucracy-based governance. The required process should remain proportionate to demonstrated risk and evidence.

> **Constitutional principle:** The higher the risk, the narrower the AI autonomy and the stronger the independent human and technical controls.

## Article 7 — Transparency & Traceability

### 7.1 Material AI-assisted work must be traceable

For material artifacts and changes, it must be possible to determine that AI participated in the work, what mandate it received, which material actions it performed, which decisions remained human-owned, what outcome was produced and who accepted that outcome.

Traceability exists to preserve engineering responsibility and reconstruct the material path from delegated work to accepted result.

**Traceability concerns engineering responsibility and material actions, not keystroke attribution.**

### 7.2 Traceability must be proportional to risk

Traceability requirements must scale with the risk and materiality of the change.

Low-risk work may rely primarily on normal repository, pull-request and CI history. Higher-risk work may require additional evidence such as execution contracts, Agent Completion Reports, decision records, deviation records, approval history and release or recovery evidence.

The objective is not to maximize recorded information, but to preserve enough evidence to understand and audit the engineering outcome.

### 7.3 AI participation should be visible without requiring line-level authorship tracking

AI-SDLC does not require attribution of every source-code line to either a human or an AI system.

For material changes, AI participation should normally be identifiable at the level at which engineering responsibility is exercised, such as the change, pull request, execution package or generated artifact.

The purpose is to preserve provenance without weakening human ownership or creating misleading line-by-line authorship history.

### 7.4 Source-level AI provenance may be required for selected artifacts

Where an artifact is machine-generated, machine-managed, regenerated from a specification, subject to elevated regulatory or audit requirements, or classified as sufficiently high risk, AI provenance may also be represented directly in source code or artifact metadata.

Such provenance may identify that an artifact was generated or materially modified with AI assistance, but it must not imply reduced human accountability.

Detailed conventions for source-code comments, annotations, generated-file markers and machine-readable metadata belong to the Level 4 Engineering Playbooks.

**AI provenance describes origin and process. It does not redefine ownership.**

### 7.5 Material assumptions, decisions and deviations must be explicit

Material assumptions made during autonomous execution must not remain hidden only inside the resulting implementation.

Where relevant, the agent should explicitly surface:

* assumptions,
* material local decisions,
* deviations from the approved plan or contract,
* detected inconsistencies,
* unresolved risks,
* limitations affecting the result.

The purpose is to allow human review to focus not only on the resulting artifact but also on the reasoning boundaries within which it was produced.

### 7.6 Material changes to the correctness contract must be traceable

Where requirements, acceptance criteria, test intent, Definition of Done or other material correctness criteria are changed, it should be possible to determine:

* what changed,
* why it changed,
* what new information triggered the change,
* who authorized it.

For material changes, the final state alone is insufficient; the decision history must remain recoverable.

### 7.7 Autonomous execution should end with an Agent Completion Report

After material autonomous work, the agent should provide an execution summary covering at least:

* the scope executed,
* the areas or artifacts changed,
* validations performed,
* completion status against the defined criteria,
* material local decisions,
* deviations or unresolved issues,
* known risks,
* relevant work deliberately not performed.

This **Agent Completion Report** forms part of the evidence used for human review and later traceability.

### 7.8 Human approvals must be traceable

For material lifecycle gates, it must be possible to determine which person or accountable role accepted the outcome or authorized progression to the next stage.

This applies, as appropriate, to analysis, architecture, implementation review, acceptance, release, production GO / NO-GO, risk acceptance and exception approval.

**Accountability without traceability becomes unverifiable accountability.**

### 7.9 Multi-agent execution must preserve material provenance

Where work is performed through multiple agents, sub-agents, skills, models or external tools, the material execution chain must remain sufficiently visible to understand how the accepted artifact was produced.

This does not require storage of every internal reasoning step. It requires preservation of material provenance such as delegated scope, significant execution actors, important tool interactions and resulting artifacts.

### 7.10 Traceability does not require deterministic reproducibility

LLM-based systems may not produce an identical result when the same task is repeated, even when similar context and configuration are provided.

The goal of traceability is therefore not necessarily bit-for-bit reproduction of generated output.

The goal is **engineering reconstructability**: the ability to understand the material context, delegation, actions, decisions, validation and acceptance that led to the trusted outcome.

### 7.11 Traceability must not violate security or privacy

Auditability does not justify unrestricted retention of sensitive information.

Traceability mechanisms must avoid unnecessary storage or exposure of secrets, credentials, production data, personal data, confidential business information or other protected content.

Traceability itself must comply with applicable security, privacy and data-governance requirements.

### 7.12 Retention must be purposeful and proportional

Not every execution record requires the same retention period or level of detail.

Retention should be determined according to risk class, legal and contractual obligations, audit requirements, incident-analysis value and the expected need for future reconstruction.

Low-risk work may be sufficiently represented by normal engineering history, while critical changes may justify richer and longer-lived evidence.

### 7.13 Traceability must support learning, not only audit

Traceability exists not only to determine what happened after a failure, but also to improve the engineering system itself.

Evidence from AI-assisted work should support:

* analysis of failure modes,
* improvement of prompts and skills,
* refinement of agent contracts,
* calibration of risk controls,
* identification of rework sources,
* improvement of AI-SDLC practices.

The primary learning question after a failure should not be only “who approved this?”, but also **“what in our engineering system allowed this failure to occur?”**

> **Constitutional principle:** Material AI actions, decisions and approvals must be sufficiently transparent to understand, review and learn from the engineering outcome.

## Article 8 — Security, Privacy & Legal Compliance

### 8.1 AI is subject to the same security boundaries as any other actor

AI agents are not intelligent exceptions to established security rules.

If a human engineer is not authorized to access production data, retrieve a secret, deploy to production or modify identity and access-management settings, an AI agent acting on that engineer’s behalf must not automatically gain those capabilities.

**AI capability does not imply AI authorization.**

### 8.2 Only approved AI systems may process protected engineering information

Organizations must define which AI systems, providers, deployment models and environments are approved for processing engineering information.

Depending on the classification of the information, this may include restrictions on public or external SaaS models, requirements for enterprise or private deployment, or explicit prohibitions on selected tools.

Access to an AI model through a browser or personal account does not by itself make that model an approved engineering tool.

### 8.3 Data classification applies before AI access

Information must be classified before it is made available to an AI system where classification is required by organizational policy.

Relevant categories may include:

* public information,
* internal information,
* confidential information,
* restricted information,
* personal data,
* secrets,
* production data.

The classification determines which AI systems, processing locations, tools and workflows may access the information.

An agent must not independently decide that protected data may be transmitted to an external model merely because doing so would simplify the task.

### 8.4 Secrets must not become ordinary model context

Credentials, API keys, passwords, access tokens, private keys, certificates and similar secrets must not be treated as ordinary AI context.

Where possible, AI workflows should operate on secret references, scoped credentials or controlled execution interfaces rather than exposing secret values directly to the model.

**Access to a repository or terminal does not imply permission to ingest every accessible secret into model context.**

### 8.5 Least privilege applies to agents and tools

AI agents should receive only the minimum capabilities and permissions required for their current delegated task.

This principle applies, as relevant, to:

* repositories,
* file systems,
* command execution,
* network access,
* cloud platforms,
* Jira and Confluence,
* databases,
* CI/CD systems,
* production environments.

Permissions should follow the delegated authority defined by the AI-SDLC rather than the maximum technical capabilities of the tool.

### 8.6 Production access requires exceptional control

Development agents should not, by default, have unrestricted write access to production systems.

AI may analyze approved telemetry, prepare remediation actions, recommend rollback, generate deployment instructions or execute narrowly defined automated procedures where policy explicitly permits this.

Material production actions must remain subject to risk-appropriate controls, explicit authorization and the accountability rules of AI-SDLC.

Automation based on pre-approved deterministic policy is distinct from unconstrained agent discretion in production.

### 8.7 AI must treat external content as untrusted input

Content read by an agent from issues, documentation, source repositories, web pages, external dependencies, comments, files or other sources must not automatically acquire instruction authority simply because it is written as an instruction.

AI workflows must account for risks such as prompt injection, malicious embedded instructions and untrusted external content.

**Content is data unless the applicable execution policy grants it authority.**

### 8.8 External tools and sub-agents inherit security constraints

When an AI agent delegates work to a sub-agent, external model, skill or tool, security boundaries must propagate through the delegation chain.

Data-classification rules, scope limitations, network restrictions, permissions and other security controls must not be weakened merely because execution crosses an agent or tool boundary.

Delegation may narrow security authority. It must not silently expand it.

### 8.9 AI-generated code must meet the same security standard as human-written code

Code generated or modified by AI is neither automatically insecure nor automatically trustworthy.

It must satisfy the same applicable engineering and security standards as human-written code, including risk-appropriate combinations of dependency scanning, static analysis, secret scanning, security review, threat analysis and testing.

Higher-risk AI-assisted changes may require stronger controls where justified by the risk model or evidence.

### 8.10 Intellectual property and licensing remain engineering constraints

Use of GenAI does not remove organizational responsibility for intellectual-property rights, software licenses, third-party materials, customer confidentiality or contractual restrictions.

AI-generated and AI-assisted artifacts must satisfy the same applicable IP, licensing and contractual requirements as other engineering artifacts.

Specific ownership or licensing treatment may depend on jurisdiction, contractual terms and the AI provider or tool in use; AI-SDLC does not assume a universal ownership rule.

### 8.11 Sensitive or regulated domains may impose stricter controls

Legal, regulatory, contractual or domain-specific obligations may require stricter limitations on AI access, processing, autonomy, validation or retention.

Such requirements override convenience and may reduce the level of AI autonomy otherwise allowed by the general risk model.

### 8.12 Security controls may impose hard stop conditions

Certain policy violations may require unconditional termination of an AI workflow regardless of a local human instruction.

Examples may include:

* unauthorized secret exposure,
* prohibited production operations,
* unauthorized transmission of protected data,
* violation of mandatory regulatory or security controls.

Human Decision Authority operates within organizational security, legal and compliance boundaries.

An individual engineer must not override a mandatory hard-stop control without the authority defined by the applicable governance process.

### 8.13 Security must be designed into AI workflows, not added after execution

AI security must be established before and during execution rather than applied only as a review of the final result.

AI workflows should therefore incorporate, as applicable:

**data classification → permissions → tool boundaries → controlled execution → security validation**

Security is part of agent and workflow design, not only a post-execution quality gate.

### 8.14 Security and compliance controls must remain auditable

For material AI-assisted operations, it should be possible to determine, where required by risk or policy:

* which permissions the agent possessed,
* which material systems or tools it accessed,
* whether protected data was transferred across a controlled boundary,
* which material actions were performed,
* which human or policy authorization permitted those actions.

Audit mechanisms must themselves comply with the data-minimization, privacy and secret-handling requirements defined in this article and in Article 7.

> **Constitutional principle:** AI operates within security, privacy, legal and contractual boundaries; capability never constitutes authorization.

## Article 9 — Production Safety & Recoverability

### 9.1 Deployment is not the end of validation

A successful deployment does not by itself mean that a change has been successfully delivered.

A release is considered complete only after there is sufficient evidence, appropriate to its risk class, that the system has been deployed correctly, remains operational, preserves critical business behavior and does not introduce unacceptable technical or business degradation.

**Deployed does not mean Done.**

### 9.2 The same trusted artifact should be promoted through environments

The artifact that has been built and verified should, where technically feasible, be the same immutable artifact promoted through subsequent environments and ultimately to production.

AI-SDLC therefore prefers:

**build once → verify → promote**

over independently rebuilding the application for each environment.

The purpose is to ensure that the artifact deployed to production is the artifact that was actually validated and accepted.

### 9.3 Production deployment requires an explicit release gate

Before a material production deployment, a risk-appropriate **Ready for Production** gate must verify, as applicable, that:

* the correct trusted artifact is being promoted,
* required tests and quality gates have passed,
* configuration and required secrets are ready,
* migrations have been verified,
* observability is available,
* the deployment strategy is defined,
* the recovery strategy is defined,
* known risks have been explicitly accepted by the appropriate human owner.

Low-risk releases may satisfy this gate primarily through automation. Higher-risk releases may require explicit human authorization.

### 9.4 Every material release requires a credible recovery strategy

Before a material release enters production, the team must be able to answer:

**What will we do if the deployment or resulting behavior is not acceptable?**

A recovery strategy may include, depending on the system and failure mode:

* aborting the rollout,
* disabling a feature through a feature flag,
* application rollback,
* configuration rollback,
* data restoration or corrective data handling,
* traffic switching,
* roll-forward deployment,
* other system-specific mechanisms.

**Absence of a credible recovery strategy is itself a material release risk.**

### 9.5 Rollback is not always the correct recovery mechanism

Not every change can or should be safely reversed by redeploying a previous application version.

Examples may include irreversible data transformations, externally emitted events, financial operations, interactions with external systems and destructive schema changes.

AI-SDLC therefore distinguishes between rollback and the broader concept of recoverability.

**Recovery capability is mandatory for material releases. Rollback capability is context-dependent.**

### 9.6 Data changes must be designed for recoverability

Material data and schema changes should be designed with coexistence, compatibility and recovery in mind.

Where appropriate, AI-SDLC prefers compatibility-oriented migration patterns such as:

**expand → migrate → observe → contract**

rather than destructive one-step changes that make rollback or coexistence with the previous application version impossible.

A change that deliberately removes safe rollback capability must be treated as an explicit risk decision appropriate to its risk class.

### 9.7 Deployment strategy must match risk

Deployment strategy should be selected according to the risk and blast radius of the change.

Applicable mechanisms may include:

* rolling deployment,
* blue/green deployment,
* canary deployment,
* progressive rollout,
* feature flags.

As potential impact increases, deployment should increasingly favor mechanisms that limit exposure and provide observable checkpoints before full rollout.

### 9.8 Feature activation and code deployment may be separate decisions

Where appropriate, deploying code and enabling new business behavior should be treated as separate decisions.

A preferred pattern may be:

**code deployed → technical verification → feature enabled for limited scope → observation → progressive enablement**

This separation can reduce risk by allowing a technically valid deployment to remain inactive until the business behavior is intentionally enabled.

In many cases, feature rollback may be faster and safer than application rollback.

### 9.9 Production smoke must verify critical viability

Immediately after deployment, a short, reliable production smoke suite should verify that the system remains viable and that its most critical paths continue to operate.

Depending on the system, this may include:

* health and readiness,
* basic service communication,
* critical APIs,
* authentication,
* access to required data stores,
* critical business journeys,
* critical external integrations.

Smoke testing is not intended to replace full regression testing. Its purpose is to answer quickly whether the system is alive and its critical behavior remains available.

### 9.10 Production observation is part of the release

A green deployment and smoke suite do not by themselves establish that a release is healthy.

A risk-appropriate observation period should examine relevant technical and business indicators, such as:

* error rate,
* latency,
* HTTP 5xx responses,
* saturation and resource behavior,
* database behavior,
* queue lag and failed events,
* operational alerts,
* business KPIs.

Where possible, the release should be evaluated using **before-release versus after-release** comparison rather than isolated post-deployment values.

### 9.11 Release acceptance is an explicit decision

For material changes, the release should reach a distinct **Release Accepted** state only after sufficient production evidence confirms that:

* deployment completed successfully,
* required smoke tests passed,
* no unacceptable degradation was detected during observation,
* relevant technical and business indicators remain within accepted boundaries.

AI-SDLC therefore adopts the operating principle:

**Done = deployed, verified and operating correctly in production.**

### 9.12 Recovery triggers should be defined before failure occurs

For material releases, the team should define recovery or rollout-interruption criteria before the failure occurs rather than inventing them during an incident.

Triggers may include, for example:

* critical smoke failure,
* error rate exceeding an accepted threshold,
* material business KPI degradation,
* failed migration,
* data-integrity violation,
* critical security or operational signal.

As risk increases, the trigger, decision owner, acceptable response time and recovery mechanism should become increasingly explicit.

### 9.13 Recovery must be tested where risk justifies it

A documented recovery or rollback plan is not equivalent to a demonstrated recovery capability.

For sufficiently high-risk releases, recovery should be exercised in a representative environment where practical, for example:

**deploy N → deploy N+1 → verify → recover to a safe state → verify**

If recovery cannot be tested before production, the remaining uncertainty must be treated as an explicit release risk.

### 9.14 AI may assist production operations, but production authority remains bounded

AI may support production operations by:

* analyzing telemetry,
* correlating failures with a release,
* recommending rollout interruption or recovery,
* preparing remediation actions,
* assisting diagnosis,
* executing narrowly defined procedures that have been explicitly authorized by policy.

This does not grant an AI agent unrestricted authority to perform material production actions.

Production autonomy must remain bounded by risk class, policy, technical permissions, pre-defined guardrails and the human accountability rules of AI-SDLC.

### 9.15 Production failures must close the lifecycle loop

Incidents, rollbacks, failed deployments and material production degradation must feed back into the engineering lifecycle.

Relevant evidence should contribute to, as appropriate:

* incident records,
* engineering and business metrics,
* lessons learned,
* backlog changes,
* improved test coverage,
* updated playbooks,
* recalibrated risk controls,
* evolution of AI-SDLC itself.

The objective is not only to restore service, but to reduce the probability and impact of recurrence.

> **Constitutional principle:** A change is not safely delivered merely because it can be deployed. It must also be observable, controllable and recoverable.

**Operating rule:** No material production release without a credible recovery strategy.

## Article 10 — Continuous Evaluation & Evolution

### 10.1 AI adoption is not a success metric

The purpose of AI-SDLC is not to maximize the amount of AI used in software delivery.

Metrics such as percentage of AI-generated code or number of agent executions are not, by themselves, measures of engineering success.

Success must be evaluated through engineering outcomes such as delivery speed, quality, reliability, rework, security, maintainability, cost and preservation of team capability.

**More AI does not automatically mean better engineering.**

### 10.2 AI-SDLC must be evidence-driven

Decisions to expand, reduce or redesign the use of AI should be based on evidence and operational experience rather than vendor marketing, organizational fashion, isolated demonstrations or subjective impressions of speed.

AI-SDLC practices should be able to answer a simple question:

**Does this practice improve the engineering outcome?**

### 10.3 Evaluation must be multidimensional

No single metric is sufficient to determine whether an AI-assisted workflow is successful.

A reduction in cycle time, for example, may be misleading if it is accompanied by increased escaped defects, review effort, rework or production failures.

Evaluation should therefore consider multiple dimensions, including at least:

* delivery,
* quality,
* reliability,
* cost,
* human engineering capability.

### 10.4 AI-assisted delivery should be compared against a meaningful baseline

Claims of productivity improvement should, where reasonably possible, be evaluated against a meaningful baseline.

A baseline may come from historical team data, similar work items, pilot periods, controlled workflow comparisons or other relevant reference points.

The objective is not laboratory precision, but to avoid replacing evidence with **productivity by feeling**.

### 10.5 Quality gains must not be traded silently for speed

AI may accelerate implementation, but faster delivery is not sufficient evidence of improvement if it causes increased rework, harder review, lower maintainability, more incidents or reduced human understanding.

Any material trade-off between speed, quality, risk or maintainability must remain visible and consciously accepted.

**Speed is one engineering outcome, not the engineering outcome.**

### 10.6 Cognitive ownership and engineering capability must be evaluated

AI-SDLC must evaluate not only software output but also whether the engineering team retains the capability required to understand, maintain and evolve the system.

Relevant signals may include changes in:

* system knowledge,
* debugging capability,
* architectural reasoning,
* domain knowledge,
* review quality,
* ability to explain and defend engineering decisions.

A process that delivers more features while progressively reducing engineering understanding is not considered a successful AI augmentation model.

### 10.7 Human feedback is valid evidence

Not every relevant effect of AI-assisted work can be captured through automated telemetry.

Structured and recurring human observations remain valid evidence, for example:

* AI-generated changes taking materially longer to review,
* agents performing especially well for specific categories of testing,
* recurring agent failure patterns in particular integrations,
* excessive escalation caused by a playbook or policy.

Such feedback should contribute to evaluation and improvement of AI-SDLC.

### 10.8 AI models and tools are changing dependencies

AI models, providers and agent platforms must be treated as changing engineering dependencies rather than stable deterministic tools.

Behavior may change because of updates to:

* models,
* providers,
* system instructions,
* context limits,
* tool-use behavior,
* agent runtimes,
* skills,
* integrations.

**AI behavior must not be assumed to remain stable merely because the workflow definition did not change.**

### 10.9 Material changes to AI capability require re-evaluation

A material change in the AI capability used by a workflow may alter quality, risk, autonomy, failure modes, security assumptions or cost.

Examples include changing the underlying model or expanding an agent from read-only analysis to write access, command execution, network access or deployment authority.

Such changes must be treated as changes to the engineering system and re-evaluated according to their impact.

### 10.10 New AI capabilities should be introduced through controlled experimentation

Material new AI capabilities should be introduced incrementally rather than through unrestricted immediate adoption.

A preferred pattern is:

**experiment → limited scope → measure → learn → adjust controls → broader rollout**

The degree of formality depends on risk. A lightweight developer tool and a production-capable autonomous agent do not require the same introduction process.

### 10.11 Controls should be removed when evidence shows they are unnecessary

Continuous improvement does not mean continually adding new gates and controls.

Controls that repeatedly demonstrate negligible value or disproportionate cost should be simplified, redesigned or removed.

AI-SDLC aims for **lean risk management**, not process accumulation.

### 10.12 Failures are inputs for framework evolution

Escaped defects, incidents, failed agent runs, unnecessary rollbacks, poor AI decisions, excessive escalation and inefficient reviews should trigger not only local remediation but also consideration of whether AI-SDLC itself requires improvement.

Potential improvement targets may include:

* prompts,
* skills,
* execution contracts,
* test intent,
* risk classification,
* review checklists,
* release gates,
* agent permissions and boundaries.

The objective is to improve the engineering system, not only the immediate implementation.

### 10.13 Improvements should be validated, not merely introduced

Material changes to AI-SDLC practices should themselves be evaluated after introduction.

If, for example, a wider scope of autonomous implementation is allowed for a risk class, the organization should later verify whether the change improved delivery without degrading quality, risk posture or human ownership.

Framework evolution should therefore follow the same evidence-oriented discipline expected from the engineering work it governs.

### 10.14 Framework changes must remain traceable

Material changes to AI-SDLC should preserve a decision record containing, as appropriate:

* the reason for the change,
* evidence or observation supporting it,
* the decision made,
* expected outcomes,
* later evaluation results.

This history should be maintained through the AI-SDLC Change Log and Decision Records so that future teams can understand not only what the framework requires, but why it evolved.

### 10.15 Evaluation must include the total cost of AI usage

The cost of AI-assisted work is not limited to model or token consumption.

Evaluation should consider, where material:

* model and API cost,
* agent runtime,
* additional CI or compute usage,
* human supervision,
* review effort,
* rework,
* failures and remediation.

A low direct model cost does not imply a low total engineering cost, just as a higher model cost may be justified if it removes substantial mechanical work and reduces overall delivery effort.

### 10.16 AI-SDLC must have a periodic review cycle

AI-SDLC must include a recurring mechanism for reviewing whether the framework remains appropriately calibrated.

Periodic review should consider, among other things:

* whether the framework continues to improve engineering outcomes,
* whether R0–R4 controls remain proportional,
* which AI practices provide measurable value,
* where new risks are emerging,
* whether the AI toolchain or capabilities have materially changed.

The exact review cadence belongs to the implementation-level governance process rather than this Constitution.

> **Constitutional principle:** AI-SDLC exists to improve engineering outcomes, not to maximize AI usage. Every material AI practice should earn its place through evidence.

## Article 11 — Exceptions & Governance

### 11.1 AI-SDLC allows explicit exceptions

AI-SDLC does not assume that every engineering situation can be predicted in advance.

There may be cases where applying the standard process is disproportionate, technically impossible, unsuitable for the situation or incompatible with an urgent operational need.

In such cases, an explicit exception may be granted.

**Exception means explicit deviation, not silent non-compliance.**

### 11.2 An exception requires a human owner

An AI system or agent must not grant itself an exception from AI-SDLC rules.

AI may identify the need for an exception and recommend one, but every material exception requires an identifiable Human Owner, appropriate Decision Authority and conscious acceptance of the resulting risk.

### 11.3 An exception must identify what is being bypassed

A material exception must state clearly:

* which rule, control or requirement is being bypassed or modified,
* why the exception is required,
* what scope it applies to,
* how long it remains valid.

The purpose is to ensure that an exception is a deliberate engineering decision rather than an ambiguous relaxation of process.

### 11.4 Exceptions should be narrow and temporary

An exception should have the smallest practical scope and should expire when the exceptional circumstance ends.

A local exception for one incident, task, release or unavailable environment must not silently expand into a standing exemption for an entire project or team.

**Exception scope must not silently expand.**

### 11.5 An exception does not lower the underlying risk

Bypassing a required control does not reduce the inherent risk of the work.

A high-risk change performed under an exception remains high-risk. The exception represents conscious acceptance of increased exposure, not a reclassification of the change into a lower-risk category.

### 11.6 Compensating controls should be used where practical

When a standard safeguard cannot be applied, the team should consider whether another control can reduce the resulting exposure.

Examples may include:

* additional human review,
* narrower rollout,
* feature flags,
* stronger production observation,
* shorter rollback thresholds,
* additional post-deployment verification.

**Removing one safeguard should trigger consideration of another safeguard.**

### 11.7 Urgency requires a break-glass path, not abandonment of governance

Critical incidents and urgent remediation may require a reduced process, but urgency does not make AI-SDLC irrelevant.

Emergency work should follow a defined break-glass path that preserves, at minimum:

* explicit exception ownership,
* appropriate authorization,
* minimum safe controls,
* traceability,
* recoverability,
* post-action review where required.

The number of gates may be reduced. Engineering accountability must not disappear.

### 11.8 Some boundaries cannot be overridden locally

Exception authority cannot exceed the authority of the person granting the exception.

Local project or engineering roles must not override applicable law, regulation, contractual obligations, mandatory security controls or organizational hard-stop policies unless the applicable governance framework explicitly grants them that authority.

### 11.9 Higher-level rules take precedence over lower-level guidance

AI-SDLC has a hierarchy of guidance.

Higher-level constitutional principles, risk rules and governance boundaries constrain lower-level Process Specifications and Engineering Playbooks.

Lower-level guidance may specialize or operationalize higher-level principles, but it must not contradict them.

### 11.10 Repeated exceptions indicate a framework problem

If the same exception is repeatedly required, this should be treated as evidence that the underlying process, risk model or control may be incorrectly calibrated.

The appropriate long-term response may be to change the framework rather than normalize recurring exceptions.

Repeated exceptions are therefore an input into Continuous Evaluation & Evolution.

### 11.11 Material exceptions must be traceable

For a material exception, it must be possible to determine:

* who authorized it,
* when it was granted,
* which rule it affected,
* why it was necessary,
* which risk was accepted,
* which compensating controls were applied,
* when the exception expires or is closed.

Material exceptions should be treated as Decision Records within the AI-SDLC governance model.

### 11.12 Significant exceptions require post-action review

Exceptions associated with high-risk work, production incidents, security-sensitive changes, rollbacks, critical hotfixes or material failures should be reviewed after the immediate situation has been resolved.

The review should determine, as appropriate:

* whether the exception was necessary,
* whether compensating controls were sufficient,
* whether any unexpected risk materialized,
* whether AI-SDLC should be changed as a result.

The objective is learning and system improvement, not retrospective blame.

### 11.13 Governance must enable engineering, not replace engineering judgement

AI-SDLC governance exists to define ownership, decision authority, risk boundaries and minimum safeguards while preserving the ability of competent engineers to act effectively.

Governance must not degrade into process execution for its own sake, ceremonial approval or replacement of engineering judgement with checklists and committees.

**Governance exists to make autonomy safe, not to eliminate autonomy.**

### 11.14 The Constitution itself may evolve

This Constitution is a controlled but living part of AI-SDLC.

When evidence or operational experience shows that a principle is incorrect, incomplete, outdated or disproportionate, it may be changed through an explicit governance process.

Material constitutional changes should follow a traceable path such as:

**evidence or reason → proposal → review → authorized decision → documented change → later evaluation**

Changes should be recorded in the AI-SDLC Change Log and Decision Records.

> **Constitutional principle:** Exceptions must be explicit, owned, proportionate and traceable. Governance exists to enable safe engineering autonomy, not to replace engineering judgement.

The initial AI-SDLC Constitution is complete with Articles 1–11.

This version establishes the first formal governance baseline for the framework. It is considered closed for the current design iteration and may be changed only through the evolution and decision-record mechanisms defined above.
